The Solution

NexaVault — A Two-Layer AI Decision System

Instead of replacing judgment with pure machine learning (which has its own risks), I built a two-layer architecture that mirrors how the best human underwriters actually think — but does it in 200 milliseconds, every time, with a full audit trail.

Layer 1 applies 9 non-negotiable business rules. Layer 2 runs a trained ML model. No credit score, however perfect, can override a failed business rule. This is how real banks should work.

The Method

How I Trained the Model

The system was trained on 2,000 loan applications generated with realistic US income distributions, Experian credit tier distributions, and real lending approval logic based on CFPB and FHA underwriting guidelines.

⚠ The Bug I Fixed (And Why It Matters)

An early version of the model was doing something backwards — high-income applicants were being rejected and low-income ones were being approved. The cause? Loan amounts were generated as a multiple of income, so rich applicants always had enormous loan-to-income ratios. The fix was simple but critical: loan amounts must be independent of income. Income determines whether you can afford the loan — it doesn't determine how much you apply for.

Feature Engineering — The Secret Weapon

Raw data doesn't teach models much. The real signal comes from engineered features — ratios and transforms that capture the economic relationships lenders actually care about.

01
Income / EMI Ratio

Primary affordability signal. If your monthly income doesn't exceed your loan payment by 1.2×, it's a hard reject regardless of credit score.

Monthly Income ÷ EMI
02
DTI Ratio

The CFPB's most important lending metric. Total monthly debt as % of gross income. Above 43% is a caution zone. Above 50% is a hard reject.

(Debt + EMI) ÷ Income
03
Affordability Score

What's actually left after paying existing debts AND the new loan? This is the residual income — what you live on.

Net Income ÷ EMI
04
Log(Income)

Income distributions are heavily right-skewed — a few very high earners distort the model. Log transform normalises this for better learning.

log(1 + Total Income)
05
Loan-to-Income

How many years of your income does this loan represent? Above 10× is a hard reject. Above 5× needs strong compensating factors.

Loan Amount ÷ Annual Income
06
Collateral Score

Real Estate (3) → Portfolio (2) → Vehicle (1) → Unsecured (0). Quality of backing directly reduces risk and improves recovery in default scenarios.

Ordinal quality scale 0–3
Model Selection

Why Gradient Boosting Won

I trained and evaluated four different algorithms on the same data using 5-fold stratified cross-validation. Here's why I use ROC-AUC instead of accuracy: a model that always predicts "Approved" would get 66% accuracy on this dataset — but would be completely useless. ROC-AUC measures whether the model can actually rank good applicants above bad ones.

Model Performance Comparison
ROC-AUC, F1 Score, Precision and Recall — holdout test set (400 records)

Gradient Boosting won because it builds trees sequentially — each tree corrects the errors of the previous one. For structured tabular data like loan applications, this iterative error correction is hard to beat. It also gives us native feature importance scores, which matter for regulatory explainability.

Notice that Income/EMI Ratio is the single most important feature — nearly 4× more important than credit score. This confirms that the model learned real lending logic: it doesn't matter how good your credit is if you literally can't afford the payment.

Analysis

Does the Model Make Sense?

A model with 99.77% AUC is impressive — but the most important question is: did it learn the right things? A model can have high accuracy while learning completely backwards signals. I verified three critical relationships.

Income vs. Approval Rate
Higher income should mean higher approval — confirmed ✓
Monthly Debt vs. Approval Rate
Higher debt should mean lower approval — confirmed ✓
Credit Tier vs. Approval Rate
Better credit should mean higher approval — confirmed ✓

All three signal directions are correct. The model learned that higher income → more approval, higher debt → less approval, better credit → more approval. This is how real lending should work. Earlier versions of the model had these signals inverted — that's what motivated the full data pipeline rewrite.

Key Takeaways

What I Learned Building This

1. Rules + ML beats pure ML

A machine learning model trained on biased data will learn biased patterns. Combining hard business rules with ML gives you the best of both — unbreakable constraints AND nuanced pattern recognition.

2. Signal direction is more important than accuracy

A 99% accurate model that learned the wrong direction is worse than useless — it's actively harmful. Always validate that your model's signals make real-world sense before shipping it.

3. Explainability is a feature, not an afterthought

The most common question after a rejection isn't "what was my score?" — it's "what do I need to change?" Building the answer into the system costs almost nothing and creates enormous user trust.

4. Open source stacks can match enterprise systems

Python, scikit-learn, Flask, and Docker are free. The entire NexaVault system was built, trained, and deployed for roughly $200K in engineering time — against enterprise systems costing $1M+. The gap is in engineering judgment, not tooling budget.
